## 1. Macro regime: growth, inflation, policy rate, credit, FX, reserves, debt, IMF, Eurobonds, rating

### Takeaway
Kenya in late 2026 is a stabilised but fiscally stretched B/B3 frontier economy. Growth is about 5%, inflation is 6.8% and rising because of a Middle East oil shock, the policy rate is on hold at 8.75%, and the shilling has been flat near KES 129-130/USD for more than two years. Reserves are about USD 14.7bn (5.9 months of imports). The binding constraints are fiscal: debt is about 68-71% of GDP, debt service takes about 70% of revenue, there is no IMF programme, and the government owes suppliers about KES 466bn in pending bills.

### Inferences
- With US 10-year yields at roughly 4-4.5% (not verified in this session), the 2034 and 2039 Eurobond yields of about 8.5-9.9% imply a USD sovereign spread of about 400-550bps. That is consistent with a B/B3 rating and a market that no longer prices imminent default, unlike 2023-early 2024.
- Real policy rate: CBR 8.75% minus 6.8% inflation gives about +2%. Real lending rate: 14.4% minus 6.8% gives about +7.6%. Bank credit is expensive in real terms, and NPLs near 14% show high borrower distress.
- The shilling's stability (about KES 129-130 since mid-2024) is partly managed, with reserves being drawn since July. The combination of a fiscal squeeze, rising oil imports, softer remittances, no IMF anchor and the Aug 2027 election points to depreciation risk skewed to the downside for the KES in 2027. This is an inference; no forecaster's KES path was found.
- Pending bills of about KES 466bn are a direct counterparty risk for any business that sells to government, whether national or county.

## 2. Passive-return benchmarks and the effective hurdle rate (KES and USD)

### Takeaway
A KES investor can earn about 8.75-9.0% gross on T-bills (about 7.4-7.7% net after the standard 15% withholding tax), 11.75-12.74% tax-free on infrastructure bonds, about 9.3% in money-market funds, and about 6-10% gross rental yields on property. The NSE returned about 29-32% YTD in 2026 with a 5.8% dividend yield. With the KES stable, a tax-free infrastructure bond paid about 12% in USD terms in 2025-26. On a forward-looking basis that assumes 3-5% annual depreciation, the risk-free USD hurdle is about 7-9%. A risky operating business should target at least 25-30% KES IRR, or about 20%+ in USD, to be compensated.

### Inferences
**Hurdle-rate construction (inference, not sourced forecasts)**
- KES risk-free: 364-day T-bill 9.04% gross, about 7.7% net of the 15% WHT. The WHT rate is standard Kenyan law from prior knowledge and was not re-verified in this session. Tax-free infrastructure bond about 12-12.7%. Real return (less 6.8% CPI): about +1% on T-bills and about +5-6% on infrastructure bonds. **The infrastructure bond, at about 12.5% tax-free, is the binding KES passive benchmark.**
- USD conversion: realised 2025-26 depreciation was about 0%, so the realised USD return on infrastructure bonds was about 12%. On a forward basis, a PPP-style drift (Kenya CPI about 6-7% vs US about 2.5-3%) implies about 3.5-4.5% a year of depreciation. An election-year stress tail (2017 and 2022 cycles saw episodes of KES weakness) suggests modelling 4-6% a year over 2027-28. **Expected USD return on the KES risk-free asset: about 6.5-9%.** That brackets Kenya's USD Eurobond yield of 8.5-9.9% at 2034-2039 maturities, a useful cross-check: the market prices KES and USD sovereign risk at roughly similar USD-equivalent returns.
- Equity benchmark: NASI's 5.8% dividend yield plus nominal earnings growth of about 7-9% implies an expected KES equity return of about 13-15%. That is only a modest premium over tax-free infrastructure bonds after a 30% rally. Property is weak by comparison: gross rental yields of 7-8.5% plus land appreciation of about 1-3% a year give about 8-11% gross total return before vacancy, maintenance, the 7.5% residential rental income tax regime and illiquidity. It underperforms the tax-free infrastructure bond.
- **Suggested hurdles for a new operating business** (inference): (a) a cash-generative SME with operating history: at least 20-25% KES equity IRR, about 15-20% in USD. (b) A startup or greenfield business with execution risk: 30-40% KES, 25%+ USD. These reflect the risk-free rate of about 12.5%, the bank cost of debt of 14.4%+, a B-rated country risk premium of about 5-7%, a small-company and illiquidity premium, and the very high early-stage MSME failure rates in section 7. Businesses selling to government should add a further premium or price in 6-18 month receivable delays, given the KES 466bn of pending bills.

## 3. Tax and regulatory environment

### Takeaway
The headline corporate rate stays at 30%. Finance Acts in 2025 and 2026 avoided big-bang rate rises after the 2024 protests and instead broadened the base and tightened compliance: eTIMS invoice matching, SEP tax on non-resident digital income, five-year loss carry-forward caps, more withholding taxes, and the removal of some sector concessions such as the 15% rate for affordable-housing developers. Foreigners can hold only 99-year leaseholds. SEZ incentives are capped at 10 years.

### Cited Findings
- FACT: The **Finance Act 2026** was assented on 23 Jun 2026, mostly effective 1 Jul 2026, with some measures from 1 Sep 2026 and 1 Jan 2027. Measures:
  - 1.5% WHT on scrap-metal sales, and a 20% WHT on gambling winnings reinstated.
  - Non-resident landlords must register, file monthly and pay a final tax by the 20th.
  - The 15% CIT rate for developers building 100+ residential units a year is removed, returning them to 30%.
  - The non-resident extractive-contractor rate falls from 37.5% to 30% from 1 Jan 2027.
  - A 100% first-year allowance for petroleum/gas storage facilities over KES 10bn.
  - Card interchange and merchant fees are treated as management/professional fees for WHT, and some payment services lose their VAT exemption.
  - 5% excise on betting-wallet deposits, and KES 40/kg on specified imported sugar.
  - Penalty and interest amnesty on liabilities to 31 Dec 2025 if the principal is paid by 31 Dec 2026.
  - Country-by-country reporting changes.
  Sources: [Legal500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/kenya/tax/finance-act-2026-tax-alert-on-notable-changes), [Academy of Tax Law](https://academyoftaxlaw.com/kenya-finance-act-2026-key-tax-changes-explained-analysis), [Pulse](https://www.pulse.co.ke/story/finance-act-2026-takes-effect-the-tax-changes-now-shaping-kenyans-and-businesses-2026093019280862417), [EY](https://globaltaxnews.ey.com/news/2026-1445-kenya-enacts-finance-act-2026), [KPMG](https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2026/07/kenya-tax-measures-finance-act-2026-cbc-reporting.html)
- FACT: The **Finance Act 2025** dropped the proposal giving KRA access to trade secrets and customer data. It introduced 0.5% (resident) and 5% (non-resident) WHT on goods supplied to public entities, replaced winnings tax with a 5% tax on withdrawals (reversed in 2026), introduced advance pricing agreements from 1 Jan 2026, capped tax-loss carry-forward at 5 years, and raised the tax-free per diem from KES 2,000 to KES 10,000 — [KPMG](https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2025/07/kenya-tax-changes-finance-act-2025.html), [Legal500](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/kenya/tax/a-summary-of-key-implications-of-the-finance-act-2025-amendments), [EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2025-1498-kenya-enacts-finance-act-2025)
- FACT: **Significant Economic Presence Tax (SEPT)**: the Tax Laws (Amendment) Act 2024 replaced the 1.5% DST with SEPT, effective 27 Dec 2024. SEPT is 30% CIT on deemed profit of 10% of turnover, an effective 3% of gross revenue for non-residents without a permanent establishment. The Finance Act 2025 reportedly removed the KES 5m threshold and widened the scope to all income from services over electronic networks. SEPT regulations were gazetted on 18 Sep 2026 — [EY](https://taxnews.ey.com/news/2026-2151-kenya-publishes-significant-economic-presence-tax-regulations-new-compliance-framework-for-nonresident-digital-service-providers), [US State Dept 2026 ICS](https://www.state.gov/reports/2026-investment-climate-statements/kenya), [tech-ish](https://tech-ish.com/2026/07/28/kra-sept-digital-tax-doubled-2026/)
- FACT: A 15% **minimum top-up tax** (Pillar Two-style) applies to groups with turnover of EUR 750m or more. It was introduced by the Tax Laws (Amendment) Act 2024 — [US State Dept 2026 ICS](https://www.state.gov/reports/2026-investment-climate-statements/kenya)
- FACT: **eTIMS**: in 2026 KRA strictly enforces e-invoicing, and input VAT and expense deductions are claimable only against valid eTIMS invoices — [biasharaguide](https://biasharaguide.co.ke/articles/significant-economic-presence-tax-kenya/) (secondary). Corporate tax is 30% — [Chambers Corporate Tax 2026 Kenya](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/corporate-tax-2026/kenya)
- FACT: **Business Laws (Amendment) Act 2024** caps SEZ incentives at 10 years from licence issue. In Nov 2024 the government moved to align the EPZ and SEZ regimes in most respects except VAT. A proposed SEZ (Amendment) Bill 2026 would extend SEZ incentives to upstream and midstream petroleum (proposal only) — [US State Dept 2026 ICS](https://www.state.gov/reports/2026-investment-climate-statements/kenya), [Ashitiva Advocates](https://ashitivaadvocates.com/insights/real-estate-and-construction/)
- FACT: **Investment Promotion Act 2004 / KenInvest**: incentives and Investment Certificate benefits, including expatriate entry permits, require a minimum foreign investment of USD 100,000. The Foreign Investment Protection Act guarantees repatriation of capital and dividends — [US State Dept 2026 ICS](https://www.state.gov/reports/2026-investment-climate-statements/kenya), [2025 ICS PDF](https://www.state.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/638719_2025-Kenya-Investment-Climate-Statement.pdf)
- FACT: **Land**: foreigners may hold only leasehold, of up to 99 years (renewable), under the Constitution. Under the Land Control Act, agricultural land cannot be transferred to foreigners without a presidential exemption — [US State Dept 2026 ICS](https://www.state.gov/reports/2026-investment-climate-statements/kenya)

### Inferences
- Policy direction is steady base-broadening and compliance tightening, which raises effective tax rates for formal businesses. eTIMS makes informal-supplier chains costly, since input VAT and expenses are disallowed. Expect each annual Finance Bill to add measures, with the pre-election FY2027/28 bill (June 2027) a protest flashpoint.
- Tax-policy volatility is high: winnings tax was 20%, then 5% on withdrawals, then 20% again within 2 years, and the developer rate went from 15% back to 30%. Business models that rely on a specific concession should be stress-tested for its removal.

### Gaps
- The main headline rates were not re-verified this session: dividend WHT (resident 5%, non-resident 15%), interest WHT, the 37.5% branch rate, and the 25% rate for newly listed companies. Check KRA.
- No 2026 primary documentation of eTIMS rollout milestones or penalty rates was found.
- The Business Laws (Amendment) Act 2024's other provisions (e.g. ownership changes in insurance and other sectors, eCitizen payments) were not covered by sources retrieved.
- No source on the 2026 Finance Bill's revenue target vs the 2025 bill was retrieved. G4S said only that it was "significantly higher".

## 4. Trade regime: AGOA, US deal, China, EU EPA, EAC/AfCFTA

### Takeaway
AGOA lapsed on 30 Sep 2025 and has been restored and extended to 31 Dec 2028, which preserves Kenya's EPZ apparel exports. No US-Kenya bilateral deal exists, and a 10% US tariff was reported in 2025. Kenya now has duty-free access to China under an "Early Harvest" deal from 1 May 2026 to 30 Apr 2028, plus full duty-free, quota-free access to the EU under the EPA.

### Cited Findings
- FACT: AGOA expired on 30 Sep 2025, was restored retroactively by a one-year extension signed in early 2026, and was then extended to **31 Dec 2028**, signed by President Trump on about 2-4 Sep 2026. The third-country fabric rule is retained. Apparel is about 70% of Kenya's exports to the US — [The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/business/2026-09-05-trump-signs-agoa-extension-hands-kenya-exporters-lifeline-to-2028), [Citizen Digital](https://citizen.digital/article/relief-for-kenyan-exporters-as-us-extends-agoa-to-2028-n389614), [Kenya Times](https://thekenyatimes.com/latest-kenya-times-news/agoa-extended-duty-free/)
- FACT: The Biden-era US-Kenya STIP talks are "no longer in place" (CS Mudavadi). A bilateral deal is being discussed, and a 10% US tariff on Kenya was reported in late 2025 — [Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/business/kenya-eyes-new-bilateral-trade-deal-with-us-agoa-extension--5288680)
- FACT: The **China zero-tariff "Early Harvest"** deal was announced on 15 Jan 2026, took effect on 1 May 2026 and reportedly runs to 30 Apr 2028. It covers tea, coffee, avocados, macadamia, flowers, leather and processed agricultural products. Reported early results: avocado-oil exports to China up 700% and coffee nearly doubled (Oct 2026). Kenya imported USD 4.31bn from China in 2024 and exported USD 196.6m — [HKTDC](https://research.hktdc.com/en/article/MjI2NDc3NDY5Mg), [Citizen Digital](https://citizen.digital/article/kenyas-exports-to-china-surge-after-zero-tariff-deal-n391240), [The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/business/2026-05-16-zero-tariffs-kenyas-trade-opportunity-in-the-chinese-market)
- FACT: The EU-Kenya EPA has applied bilaterally since 1 Jul 2024. The EU gives duty-free, quota-free access from day one, except arms, and Kenya liberalises its own tariffs gradually (dated 2024 source) — [European Commission](https://trade.ec.europa.eu/access-to-markets/de/news/bilateral-application-eu-kenya-trade-agreement-1-july-2024)

### Inferences
- Agro-processing and export manufacturing now have preferential access to the US (to 2028), the EU (permanent) and China (to 2028). That makes EPZ/SEZ export plays more viable than in 2025, although the AGOA and China windows both end in 2028, inside a typical 5-10 year investment horizon.

## 7. Empirical evidence on firm returns and survival

### Takeaway
Evidence on Kenyan firm survival is dated but stark. The 2016 KNBS MSME survey found about 2.2m closures over 2011-16, with 46% of closed firms failing in their first year. Finance constraints (30%) and market access (20%) were the top causes. Newer data points to roughly 28% non-operational MSEs (2023) and 60%+ failure within 3 years for youth-led MSMEs (advocacy estimate).

### Cited Findings
- FACT (DATED, 2016): The KNBS MSME Survey 2016 found about 2.2m businesses closed between 2011 and 2016, and 46% of closed businesses did not pass year one. Reasons: finance (30%), personal (23%), market access (20%), stock/raw-material shortage (6%). Wholesale/retail had 53.8% of MSME employment and 45.8% of closures — [Kuza Biashara](http://www.kuzabiashara.co.ke/blog/why-most-small-businesses-in-kenya-barely-survive-long-enough/), [KIPPRA](https://kippra.or.ke/14355-2/)
- FACT (DATED, 2023): The FinAccess MSE Track Survey (Mar 2023, n=4,125) found 71.6% of MSEs still operational — [KNBS NADA](https://statistics.knbs.or.ke/nada/index.php/catalog/129)
- Estimate (advocacy): about 60% of youth-led MSMEs collapse within 3 years — [Streamline Feed](https://streamlinefeed.co.ke/news/why-60-percent-of-kenyan-youth-start-ups-collapse-despite-record-foreign-investment). MSMEs reportedly generate about 85% of new jobs — [Strathmore Business School](https://sbs.strathmore.edu/kenyas-msmes-power-85-of-new-jobs-yet-policy-gaps-keep-millions-of-business-from-growing/)
- Background (DATED 2024): FSD Kenya MSME outlook report — [FSD Kenya](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Micro-Small-and-Medium-Enterprises-outlook-report.pdf)

### Inferences
- Survival rates of about 50% at year one and about 40% at year three, for micro firms, imply that a portfolio-level hurdle for early-stage businesses must roughly double the target return on survivors. This supports the 30-40% KES IRR hurdle in section 2 for greenfield ventures.

### Gaps
- No World Bank Enterprise Survey (Kenya 2018, or any 2025 wave) metrics were retrieved, such as firm productivity, losses from crime and power outages, or the share citing finance as the top constraint.
- No academic return-on-capital evidence was retrieved, such as de Mel/McKenzie-style returns to capital for Kenyan microenterprises, Kenyan J-PAL/IGC studies, or KIPPRA firm-productivity papers. If needed, the report writer should label this an evidence gap rather than fill it.
- No 2025-26 KNBS business-births/deaths data was found.
