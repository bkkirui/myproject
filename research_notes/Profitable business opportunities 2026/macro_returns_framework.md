## Q1. What is the macro and capital-markets state as of October 2026 (rates, Fed, inflation, tariffs, AI capex, valuations, exits, VC/PE fundraising)?

### Takeaway
The regime of October 2026 is **stagflation-lite with a "higher-for-longer-again" rate shock**:
- An Iran war that began in late February 2026, together with disruption in the Strait of Hormuz, pushed oil to roughly $95–106. Headline CPI is 3.7%.
- The Fed under Chair Kevin Warsh *hiked* to 3.75–4.00% in September 2026 and signals another hike.
- The 10-year Treasury is above 5.2%, its highest since 2002. That leaves the S&P 500 earnings yield roughly equal to the 10-year yield, so the equity risk premium is close to zero.
- Capital is extremely concentrated in AI. AI took 82.7% of US VC dollars YTD, and hyperscaler capex is about $700B+.
- Exits are bifurcated. IPO proceeds hit a record because of SpaceX's roughly $75B IPO, while IPO counts fell about 33%. M&A had a record H1 and then fell 41% QoQ in Q3.
- PE and VC liquidity is still in a multi-year DPI drought. Distributions have been below 15% of NAV for 4 straight years, and the 2019–2021 vintages are below 0.35x DPI. That drought keeps fundraising concentrated in mega-funds.

### Inferences
- **Higher hurdle rates.** With the 10-year above 5% and policy rates rising, the risk-free hurdle for any venture or private investment is about 5%. Leveraged buyout math that relies on multiple expansion and cheap debt is impaired. Cash-generative, low-leverage businesses and the ability to lend (private credit) are relatively favored.
- **Two-speed capital market.** AI mega-rounds absorb most VC dollars, while seed and non-AI companies face scarcer capital. Non-AI ventures should plan for lower valuations and longer paths to exit, which makes capital efficiency and self-funding more valuable.
- **The DPI drought creates buyer's markets in illiquidity.** Secondaries, continuation vehicles, and buying from sellers who need liquidity are the areas where structural discounts exist. Small-company M&A (search funds, ETA) faces less competition from mega-funds, but financing costs are higher.
- **Energy-shock beneficiaries.** Energy efficiency, domestic energy supply, and power infrastructure gain from the oil shock and from AI power demand. Discretionary-consumer ventures face a real-income squeeze.
- **Downside scenario.** The near-zero equity risk premium and CAPE around 40 mean public equities price in optimism. A rate or AI-capex disappointment would compress exit multiples for all private assets, so ventures should be underwritten at exit multiples below today's.

## Q2. What does rigorous academic research find about returns to entrepreneurship, VC, PE, search funds and small-business ownership (including selection, skewness and survivorship)?

### Takeaway
The academic consensus has five parts:
1. **The typical entrepreneur earns no risk premium.** Private-business equity returns are about equal to public equity despite extreme undiversified risk. This is the "private equity premium puzzle."
2. **Venture payoffs are extremely skewed.** Founders get nothing in about 3/4 of VC-backed outcomes. For normal risk aversion, the certainty-equivalent value of founding is about zero.
3. **Buyout funds have beaten public markets by about 3%/year historically** (PME about 1.2). VC has been strongly vintage-dependent.
4. **GP-level persistence has weakened**, and it now shows up mainly at the bottom quartile.
5. **Search funds show high aggregate returns** (about 34% IRR), but these are driven by a small right tail, and about 1/3 of searchers never acquire a company.

Non-pecuniary motives (Hurst-Pugsley) and overoptimism explain much of the willingness to accept these poor risk-adjusted returns. The data favor older founders with industry experience (mean age about 45 for the top 0.1% growth firms).

### Cited Findings

**Entrepreneurship and private business equity**
- **Moskowitz & Vissing-Jørgensen (AER 2002), "The Returns to Entrepreneurial Investment: A Private Equity Premium Puzzle?"**
  - Returns on US non-public equity are about **the same as public equity returns**, even though holdings are highly concentrated and undiversified.
  - Proposed explanations: non-pecuniary benefits, a preference for skewness, and overestimated survival odds.
  — [NBER w8876](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8876); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/returns-entrepreneurial-investment)
- **Follow-up work:** a Bank of Canada study finds the premium "does not survive" outside the high-return 1990s. — [Bank of Canada, "The Private Equity Premium Puzzle Revisited" (WP 11-6)](https://ideas.repec.org:443/p/bca/bocawp/11-6.html)
- **Hall & Woodward (AER 2010), "The Burden of the Nondiversifiable Risk of Entrepreneurship"** — [NBER w14219](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14219); [IDEAS/RePEc AER 100(3):1163–94](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v100y2010i3p1163-94.html); [Harvard Law School Forum summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2009/07/29/the-risk-burden-of-entrepreneurship)
  - Founders of VC-backed companies earned an average of about **$4.4M** (published version; an earlier draft gave $3.6M).
  - The founder reward is **zero in almost three-quarters of outcomes**.
  - The certainty-equivalent value of founding is $3.6M if risk-neutral, about $0.7M with mild risk aversion and $100k savings, and **slightly negative with normal risk aversion** (CRRA = 2).
  - With CRRA = 2 and wealth below about $0.7M, a person is better off in salaried work.
  - Startups therefore select for low risk aversion, higher wealth, a preference for entrepreneurship, and optimism.
- **Hamilton (JPE 2000), "Does Entrepreneurship Pay?"** [lit. knowledge]
  - Most self-employed people have both lower initial earnings and lower earnings growth than in paid employment.
  - The median 10-year earnings gap is about 35%.
  - This points to large non-pecuniary benefits. — [JPE 108(3)](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/262131)
- **Hurst & Pugsley (Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, Fall 2011), "What Do Small Businesses Do?"** — [BPEA 2011(2):73–142](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-do-small-businesses-do/) (citation confirmed via [RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cen/wpaper/17-53.html) context)
  - [lit. knowledge] Most small businesses (plumbers, dentists, shops) have **no intention to grow or innovate**.
  - Non-pecuniary benefits such as being one's own boss and flexibility are first-order motives for over 50% of new business owners.
  - Implication: average small-business returns are not the right benchmark for growth ventures.
- **Azoulay, Jones, Kim & Miranda (AER: Insights 2020), "Age and High-Growth Entrepreneurship"** — [RePEc / Census CES WP](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cen/cpaper/2018-03.html)
  - The **mean founder age for the fastest-growing 1-in-1,000 startups is 45.0**.
  - **Prior experience in the same industry strongly predicts success.**

**Venture capital**
- [lit. knowledge] **Cochrane (JFE 2005), "The Risk and Return of Venture Capital"**
  - After correcting for selection bias (valuations are observed only at financing or exit events), VC investments have arithmetic mean returns of about 57%/yr with very high volatility (about 89%).
  - Individual-deal returns are highly skewed: log returns are much lower and the median is negative. — [JFE 75(1)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.03.006)
- **Harris, Jenkinson & Kaplan (JF 2014), "Private Equity Performance: What Do We Know?"** — [PSERS copy of paper](https://www.psers.pa.gov:443/About/Investment/Documents/PPMAIRC%202018/25%20Private%20Equity%20Performance%20-%20What%20Do%20We%20Know.pdf); [Harvard Law Forum summary](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2012/08/13/private-equity-performance)
  - The study uses Burgiss cash flows from about 1,400 US buyout and VC funds.
  - **Buyout funds beat the S&P 500 by 20–27% over fund life, more than 3%/yr.**
  - **VC beat public markets in the 1990s but underperformed in the 2000s.**
  - Earlier studies were biased by flawed Venture Economics data.
- **Harris, Jenkinson, Kaplan & Stucke (NBER w28109, 2020; Journal of Corporate Finance), "Has Persistence Persisted in Private Equity?"** — [NBER w28109](https://nber.org/papers/w28109)
  - Measured using the previous fund's PME at the time of fundraising, persistence is **modest and driven by the bottom quartile, not the top**, especially for post-2000 buyouts.
- [lit. knowledge] **Kaplan & Schoar (JF 2005), "Private Equity Performance: Returns, Persistence, and Capital Flows"**
  - Average LP returns net of fees are roughly equal to the S&P 500 (PME about 1).
  - GP returns **persist strongly** across funds.
  - Top performers grow less than proportionately. — [NBER w9807](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9807)
- [lit. knowledge] **Korteweg & Sorensen (JFE 2017), "Skill and Luck in Private Equity Performance"**
  - Their model separates long-term persistence from short-term noise.
  - They find meaningful **long-term persistence (skill), especially in VC**. But noisy fund returns make it hard for LPs to identify top GPs ex ante.
  - Top-quartile managers may beat bottom-quartile ones by 7–8 percentage points annually (figure from memory; verify). — [JFE 124(3)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2017.03.006)
- [lit. knowledge] **Kerr, Nanda & Rhodes-Kropf (JEP 2014), "Entrepreneurship as Experimentation"**
  - In Correlation Ventures data, about 55% of VC-funded investments returned less than the capital invested.
  - About 6% returned 5x or more, and those generated about 60% of total VC returns. — [JEP 28(3)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.28.3.25)

**Search funds / entrepreneurship through acquisition (ETA)**
- **Stanford GSB 2026 Search Fund Study** — [Peony](https://www.peony.ink/blog/search-fund-guide); [ClearlyAcquired](https://www.clearlyacquired.com/blog/2026-stanford-search-fund-study); [CapitalPad](https://capitalpad.com/search-fund-statistics/); [Five Experts](https://fiveexperts.com/resources/so-you-want-to-buy-a-business-what-stanfords-2026-search-fund-study-tells-aspiring-searchers) (all secondary summaries of the Stanford study)
  - Coverage: 862 US and Canadian search funds, with data through Dec 31, 2025.
  - Aggregate pre-tax **IRR 33.9%, ROI 4.75x, PME 2.88**.
  - Exited funds: 39.3% IRR, 5.98x.
  - Excluding funds that returned 10x or more: about 27% IRR, 2.8x.
  - **Excluding the top 10%: about 20% IRR, 2.1x.**
  - IRR fell from 35.1% in the 2024 study while ROI rose from 4.5x, because hold periods were longer.
  - About **1 in 3 searchers never acquire a company**, and about 1/4 of acquisitions lose money (per the Stanford primer, as reported).
- **Measurement caveat:** these are investor-level returns (pooled capital, many small checks), not searcher-level returns. Searchers earn carry and a salary, with a large dispersion of outcomes.

### Inferences
- **For an individual operator,** the base rate is that founding a business does *not* earn a risk premium. Expected financial returns roughly match an index fund but with far higher variance. A venture must clear a bar well above about 10–12% expected return to compensate for undiversifiable risk, illiquidity, and labor opportunity cost.
- **Skewness dominates.** In VC, about 6% of deals produce about 60% of returns, and founders get zero in about 75% of cases. So **position sizing and portfolio breadth** matter more than picking.
- **Single-company operators should prefer ventures with a high probability of a modest positive outcome**, such as acquisition of a cash-flowing SMB, unless they have high wealth or low risk aversion.
- **Search funds have the most attractive aggregate risk-adjusted record among the operator paths** (PME 2.88). But even without the top 10% the IRR of about 20% reflects survivors among those who closed a deal. The one-third who never acquire bear the cost.
- **Founder selection matters.** Industry experience and mid-career age predict high growth, which supports "founder-market fit" as a screening variable with empirical backing.
- **Manager selection in PE and VC:** persistence is weaker post-2000, except for avoiding bottom-quartile managers. Fees and the illiquidity of LP positions must be justified against a PME of about 1.0–1.2.

### Gaps
- Korteweg-Sorensen, Kaplan-Schoar, Cochrane, Hamilton, Kerr-Nanda-Rhodes-Kropf and Hurst-Pugsley details are from prior literature knowledge and were not re-verified this session (marked [lit. knowledge]).
- The primary Stanford 2026 study PDF was not accessed.
- Recent (2024–2026) Burgiss/MSCI PME estimates for 2015–2021 vintages were not found. Given DPI below 0.35x for 2019–2021 vintages, these vintages are likely to underperform, but final PMEs are unknown.
- No rigorous return data were found for real assets (farmland, timber, infrastructure) or for SBA 7(a)-financed SMB acquisitions in 2026.

The best-evidenced, already-funded profit pools are:
1. **AI infrastructure and power.** Hyperscaler capex is about $700B+ in 2026. US data-center power demand is forecast to rise from 31 GW (2025) to 41 GW (2026) and 66 GW (2027), with a widening grid supply gap by 2028.
2. **Housing undersupply.** Estimates of the shortage range from 1.2M to 10M units, with most mainstream estimates at 3.7–4.9M.

## Q4. Which industries have persistently high ROIC and markups, and how should an investor think about competition eroding excess returns?

### Takeaway
Markups and profits have risen and concentrated in a small set of firms:
- **US aggregate markups rose from 21% above marginal cost (1980) to 61% (2016)**, and the average profit rate rose from 1% to 8% (De Loecker, Eeckhout & Unger, QJE 2020).
- The rise came entirely from the upper tail of the distribution. **The median firm's markup did not rise.**

Industry ROIC dispersion is large and persistent (Damodaran, January 2026 data):
- Advertising: about 37% return on capital.
- Regional banks: about 0%.
- Some sectors, such as internet software, reportedly earn below their cost of capital.

The investor lesson is that excess returns accrue to firm-specific advantages, not to industries per se. Competition erodes average excess returns, but the top tail persists.

### Cited Findings
- **De Loecker, Eeckhout & Unger (QJE 2020), "The Rise of Market Power and the Macroeconomic Implications"** — [NBER w23687](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23687); [CREI PDF](https://crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/the-rise-1.pdf)
  - US firm-level data since 1955 show aggregate markups rising from **21% (1980) to 61%**.
  - The increase comes from the upper tail. **The median markup is unchanged.**
  - The average profit rate rose from **1% to 8%**.
  - The 2017 working paper reported 18% to 67%, so cite the version used.
- **Damodaran return-on-capital dataset (January 2026)** — [NYU Stern ROC](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/roc.html); [NYU Stern Margin/ROIC](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/mgnroc.html)
  - Provides pre-tax and after-tax ROIC by industry, lease- and R&D-adjusted, plus a 10-year normalized figure.
  - Examples: Advertising about 37.3% return on capital, Regional Banks about 0%.
- Secondary examples, unverified against the dataset: General Utilities about 6.0% ROC against a 4.36% cost of capital, and Internet Software about 6.2% ROC against a **10.66%** cost of capital, i.e. value-destroying in aggregate. — [StockUnlock (secondary)](https://stockunlock.com/investing/what-is-a-good-roic/)
- [lit. knowledge] **McKinsey (Koller, Goedhart & Wessels, *Valuation*, 7th ed.)** — [McKinsey "Valuation" book page](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/valuation-measuring-and-managing-the-value-of-companies)
  - Median US non-financial ROIC excluding goodwill is about 10–15% over decades.
  - Industries with brands, patents, or network effects (pharma, software, consumer staples brands) sustain high ROIC.
  - Commodity and capital-intensive industries (airlines, utilities, paper, steel) cluster near the cost of capital.
  - Company ROIC **regresses toward the median**, but high-ROIC firms decay slowly. About half of companies in the top quintile remain in the top two quintiles a decade later.

### Inferences
- **Industry choice matters, but intra-industry dispersion is larger.** The DEU result (median markup flat, top tail up) means superior returns require a firm-level moat: scale economies, switching costs, network effects, regulatory licenses, or local density.
- **"Hot" sectors can earn below their cost of capital in aggregate,** as the Internet Software example suggests if verified. Heavy competition and capital inflows (now AI) compress returns even when growth is high.
- **For small ventures,** persistent excess returns typically come from local or niche market power: route density in services, licensed trades, specialized B2B suppliers with high switching costs. These are the targets search funds historically favor.
- **Competition-erosion heuristic:** assume excess ROIC fades toward the cost of capital over 5–10 years unless a named, specific barrier exists. Capital flooding into a theme, as with AI VC at 82.7% of dollars, is a leading indicator of faster fade.

### Gaps
- Damodaran's full excess-return spreads (ROIC minus WACC) by industry were not extracted. The xls file should be downloaded.
- The McKinsey ROIC decay statistics are from prior knowledge, not re-verified.
- No 2025–2026 update to the markup literature was retrieved, e.g. critiques by Traina or Basu on cost measurement.

## Q5. What practical screening framework do top investors and economists recommend?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a screen that combines four elements:
1. **Hurdle rate:** expected return must exceed the elevated risk-free rate (about 5%) plus a premium for undiversifiable risk and illiquidity.
2. **Payoff distribution:** probability of loss, skewness, and dependence on a right tail.
3. **Moat and ROIC durability.**
4. **Founder/operator–market fit:** industry experience, with mid-career founders outperforming.

These sit on top of standard filters: market size and growth, margins, capital intensity, downside protection, and liquidity or exit path, all stress-tested for current exit multiples and financing costs.

### Cited Findings (evidence underpinning each screen dimension)
- **Hurdle rate and risk premium:**
  - 10-year Treasury above 5.2% and the Fed hiking. — [ETF Trends](https://www.etftrends.com/fixed-income-content-hub/treasury-yields-snapshot-october-9-2026/); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/16/fed-rate-decision-september-2026.html)
  - Undiversified founders need a large premium: the certainty equivalent is about zero at CRRA = 2. — [Hall & Woodward](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14219)
  - Private business returns historically about equal public equity. — [Moskowitz & Vissing-Jørgensen](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8876)
- **Payoff skewness and probability of loss:**
  - Founders get zero in about 75% of VC outcomes. — [Hall & Woodward](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2009/07/29/the-risk-burden-of-entrepreneurship)
  - Search fund aggregate returns fall from 33.9% to about 20% IRR without the top 10%, and 1/3 of searchers never acquire. — [CapitalPad](https://capitalpad.com/search-fund-statistics/); [Peony](https://www.peony.ink/blog/search-fund-guide)
- **Founder–market fit:** the mean age of top-0.1% growth founders is 45, and same-industry experience predicts success. — [Azoulay et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cen/cpaper/2018-03.html)
- **Moat and ROIC:** excess profits concentrate in top-tail firms. — [De Loecker et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23687); [Damodaran ROIC](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/datafile/roc.html)
- **Liquidity and exit path:**
  - PE DPI below 0.35x for 2019–2021 vintages, about 7-year holds, and $3.8T of unsold companies. — [Foley](https://www.foley.com/insights/publications/2026/09/private-equitys-285-billion-shock-year-still-has-real-openings/); [Bain](https://www.bain.com/about/media-center/press-releases/2026/winning-firms-will-focus-on-what-they-can-control-weather-the-rest-as-triple-shock-brakes-private-equitys-latest-revival-bain--company-2026-midyear-pe-report/)
  - IPO count down 33%. — [Renaissance](https://www.renaissancecapital.com/IPO-Center/Stats)
- **Manager or GP selection** (if investing via funds): persistence now is mainly a bottom-quartile signal. — [Harris et al. 2020](https://nber.org/papers/w28109)
- **Search fund practice** [lit. knowledge; Stanford primer]: target fragmented industries with recurring revenue, steady growth, high margins, low capital intensity, and no technology-disruption or customer-concentration risk. — [Stanford GSB search fund primer summary (secondary)](https://gosbaloans.com/guide/stanford-search-fund-primer-summary/)

### Inferences
Proposed screening scorecard (synthesized from the evidence above; weights are judgment, not empirically estimated):

| Dimension | What to measure | Evidence-based threshold / red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Hurdle | Expected unlevered IRR vs. 10Y (about 5.2%) + undiversified-risk premium | Operator ventures: target ≥20% expected IRR; reject anything that only matches public-equity returns (MVJ puzzle) |
| Payoff distribution | P(loss), P(≥3x), dependence on tail | Single-bet operators: prefer P(loss) <25% (search fund base rate); VC-style bets only as small, diversified positions |
| Market | Size, growth, fragmentation | Growth funded by committed spend (AI power, housing, care) > speculative TAM |
| Unit economics | Gross/EBITDA margin, ROIC vs. WACC, capital intensity | ROIC durably > WACC + 5pp; low capex/revenue; check Damodaran industry baseline |
| Moat | Named barrier (switching costs, density, license, network) | Assume fade to WACC within 5–10 years without one; heavy capital inflow = faster fade |
| Founder–market fit | Years of industry experience, prior operating role | Favor deep domain experience; age is not a negative (mean 45 for top growth) |
| Macro sensitivity | Rate, energy-cost, tariff, consumer-income exposure | Stress at +100bp rates, $110 oil, tariff reversal |
| Downside protection | Asset backing, recurring revenue, leverage, personal guarantees | Low leverage at 7–9% borrowing costs; recurring revenue |
| Liquidity / exit | Realistic buyer universe, hold period | Underwrite 7-year hold, exit multiple ≤ entry multiple; secondaries/continuation as fallback |

- Non-pecuniary utility (autonomy) is a legitimate part of the return for many owners (Hurst-Pugsley, Hamilton). It should be recognized explicitly rather than hidden as overoptimistic financial projections.

### Gaps
- No single peer-reviewed paper validates a weighted multi-factor screen for private ventures. The scorecard above is a synthesis.
- Published investor frameworks were not retrieved this session, e.g. Sequoia/a16z memos, Buffett/Munger moat criteria, or Mauboussin's "Measuring the Moat." They are well known, but source text was not re-verified.
- No 2026 data were found on SBA lending rates, small-business acquisition multiples (e.g. BizBuySell or GF Data), or small-business failure rates (BLS BED survival rates). These would sharpen the SMB-ownership benchmarks.
