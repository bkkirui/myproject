# Review r1 - Livestock / Animal Health (adversarial)

Scope: PLAN.md Draft v1. Tags: [MAJOR] = material loss, safety/legal exposure or plan failure. [MINOR] = fix but not fatal.

1. [MAJOR] No notifiable-disease protocol, and the owner is removed from the loop on exactly the events that carry legal duty.
   Problem: "disease signal" is an alert, and WP4 lists an "emergency playbook" with no content. Nothing says who reports to the state/national veterinary authority, within what time, or that nobody may move animals, carcasses, milk, eggs, manure or vehicles meanwhile. Suspect FMD, anthrax, brucellosis, bovine TB, PPR, sheep/goat pox, RVF, HPAI, Newcastle disease, ASF if pigs are nearby, and rabies are the cases that matter, with country-specific lists.
   Why it matters: suspicion alone is reportable in most jurisdictions. Late or non-reporting brings prosecution, loss of compensation, movement bans on the whole holding, and a worker opening an anthrax carcass or handling an aborted fetus (brucellosis, Q fever, RVF) risks death.
   Fix: Name the competent authority and its 24-hour contact in the manual. Write a "suspect list" with trigger signs (multiple sudden deaths, vesicles/drooling/lameness, abortion storm, neurological cases, bloody discharge from a carcass, a poultry mortality jump). The first action is to report to the vet and the authority and then isolate. Do not open sudden-death carcasses. Put a standstill rule on all movement. Make the owner and the vet automatic same-hour notifications. This is a legal duty and cannot be delegated away from the licensed holder.

2. [MAJOR] Zoonosis and worker safety are absent.
   Problem: no PPE, no brucellosis/Q fever/leptospirosis/orf/Rift Valley/avian flu/Campylobacter/Cryptosporidium controls, no raw-milk rule, no abortion-handling SOP, no pregnant-worker policy, no needle-stick or animal-handling injury plan. Cattle handling (bulls, cows with calves) is the top cause of farm fatalities.
   Why it matters: injury, death and liability, with an unsupervised workforce and no owner on site.
   Fix: Add a zoonosis SOP (gloves/masks for abortions, disposal in the designated pit, no raw milk, pasteurise), induction training, a first-aid and emergency-transport plan, a rule that nobody works alone with bulls or in a crush, and workers' compensation or equivalent cover.

3. [MAJOR] Biosecurity and quarantine are named but not designed, and mixed species and poultry make them harder.
   Problem: a "quarantine SOP" is listed with no standards. Needed: 21-30 day isolation of all incoming animals, with test and treat (BVD, Johne's, brucellosis, TB, caseous lymphadenitis, footrot, resistant worms, Mycoplasma, CAE and Johne's in goats), separate staff and boots, a visitor and vehicle log, and wild-bird and rodent control at poultry.
   Why it matters: bought-in stock are the main route for disease. Poultry and ruminants sharing staff, vehicles and feed stores spread HPAI, Salmonella and Newcastle. Having bought-in animals and "AI/bull" in the same plan guarantees introductions.
   Fix: A written introduction rule (source herd status, health certificate, test, quarantine away from the main flock), staff flow from youngest/cleanest to oldest/sickest, one-way poultry zone with a hard door, and a visitor logbook that the manager signs off.

4. [MAJOR] The plan lumps sheep, goats and cattle together, but their management differs in ways that kill animals.
   Problem: "rations by class", "health program" and "grazing/rotation" are generic. Specific errors that will occur:
   - Copper: sheep die from copper toxicity on cattle or goat minerals and some poultry/pig feeds. Goats need copper and sheep do not.
   - Goats are browsers, have little innate worm resistance, are highly susceptible to Haemonchus, and need different drug dosing (many licensed sheep/cattle anthelmintics are under-dosed for goats, so resistance develops quickly). Goats do poorly on wet pasture and need shelter.
   - Sheep and goats on the same paddock share worms with cattle only partly, but sheep and goats share them with each other. Mixed grazing needs designed rotation.
   - Enterotoxaemia/clostridial disease and tetanus vaccination for all small ruminants. Pregnancy toxaemia in late-pregnant ewes and does. Urinary calculi in feeder males on high-grain rations (wethers need ammonium chloride/calcium:phosphorus control).
   - Cattle: bloat on legumes, grass tetany, milk fever and ketosis around calving, calving difficulty and calf scours/colostrum (4 L or 10% of body weight within six hours).
   - Predators (dogs, jackals, foxes, hyenas, leopards) take lambs and kids and need night housing or guardian animals.
   Why it matters: these are the common causes of heavy loss and each is preventable by a species-specific rule.
   Fix: Separate species SOP chapters, a mineral and feed rule per species ("no cattle mineral mix to sheep" stored apart and labelled), colostrum rule, kidding/lambing/calving supervision plan, predator plan, and vet sign-off on the health calendar for the local disease map.

5. [MAJOR] Worm control rests on FAMACHA alone, and "deworming" without resistance management will fail within seasons.
   Problem: FAMACHA scores anaemia from Haemonchus only. It does not cover Teladorsagia, Trichostrongylus, Nematodirus, liver fluke, coccidia, or cattle Ostertagia. It needs trained scoring every 2 to 3 weeks in the risk season by someone competent. Blanket calendar drenching is the cause of resistant populations.
   Why it matters: haemonchosis can kill a flock in 2-3 weeks, and resistance (benzimidazole, levamisole, ML, even monepantel) is already widespread.
   Fix: Targeted selective treatment with faecal egg counts and drench-resistance testing (FECRT) at set-up and annually, trained FAMACHA assessors with a documented competency check, quarantine drench for incomers, refugia, rotation, and treat only animals above the threshold. Put the FECRT and worm calendar under the vet retainer.

6. [MAJOR] Vet retainer, drugs and withdrawal-period control are thin, and an unsupervised team will misuse medicines.
   Problem: "vet-call criteria" and "withdrawal-period tracking" are named only. There is no rule on who can hold or administer prescription drugs, antibiotic stewardship, the controlled storage and cold chain for vaccines, needle and sharps handling, or off-label use (most goat drugs are off-label, so a vet must set the withdrawal). The owner cannot check this remotely.
   Why it matters: violative residues in meat, milk and eggs cause rejected consignments, fines, loss of market and consumer harm. Drug-store theft or resale by staff is a classic loss.
   Fix: A vet-of-record with a written veterinary-client-patient relationship and a treatment protocol list per condition. A locked drug register with a count reconciled monthly. Every treated animal gets a recorded withdrawal date with a physical mark and a block on sale or milking into the supply. Vaccine cold-chain log with min/max thermometer. Sharps and broken-needle rule.

7. [MAJOR] Labour assumptions are unrealistic for animals.
   Problem: animals need daily presence and skilled eyes seven days a week, with extra cover at birth, heat, flood, drought and holidays. "Run from task sheets" will not catch early signs (a ewe off by herself, a kid not suckling, a cow in first-stage labour, a goat with bloat). Task sheets for field hands do not replace stockmanship. Calving and lambing/kidding in one window will exceed the headcount. Night watch is not mentioned for lambing, predators, theft and poultry house failure. The plan also names no stockperson headcount ratio.
   Why it matters: most newborn and late-pregnancy losses occur in the first 48 hours and at night, and an unwatched farm loses 10-20%+ of the lamb/kid crop and a share of the heifers.
   Fix: Size staff to the animal-hours (rule of thumb input from scale), a trained lead stockperson per species with a documented competency check, a split birthing season that the manager can staff, a 7-day roster with named relief, a paid on-call rota, and a birthing-season surge plan. Treat labour supply as a design constraint before enterprise mix, and cut the enterprise count if it cannot be staffed.

8. [MAJOR] Too many enterprises for an absentee owner, and the single manager is an unmitigated key-person risk.
   Problem: cattle, sheep, goats, poultry and crops, each with its own expertise, run by one manager and a "deputy" who is not specified. Any one of them (layers, dairy, breeding) is a full-time specialised job. The plan keeps all of them and then adds AI, FAMACHA and IPM.
   Why it matters: the likeliest outcome is a farm that does everything poorly, and when the manager leaves or colludes, nobody can detect it. The "monthly audit" is the only check and counts stock, which can be fudged.
   Fix: Phase enterprises: start with the one or two that fit the land, labour and market, and add others only after 12 months of clean records. Require a deputy who is named and trained. Make the audit unannounced, include the vet, count live animals against ear tags and not against the register, and check feed and drug use against production (feed conversion, birth records, sale receipts). Add separation of duties: manager does not control cash, procurement and stock counts together.

9. [MAJOR] Animal ID, traceability and movement control are an afterthought, and in many countries are mandatory.
   Problem: "animal ID and records" is mentioned with no standard. No individual ear tags/EID, birth and movement register, premises registration, or movement permits. Group records for small ruminants cannot support a disease trace, the withdrawal system, the stock audit or genetics.
   Why it matters: a disease event with no traceability means the whole holding is blocked, and sale of animals without a valid movement permit is illegal in most places. Theft or "shrinkage" becomes invisible.
   Fix: Individual ID at birth for cattle, flock/herd ID plus individual for breeding small stock, registered holding number, birth/death/sale/movement register kept on the phone forms, and no animal leaves without a signed movement document.

10. [MAJOR] Death, carcass and waste disposal is a one-line item, and is both a disease and a legal exposure.
   Problem: "mortality disposal" has no method. Composting, burial depth, incineration, rendering and collection depend on local law. Anthrax and notifiable-disease carcasses cannot be opened, buried or composted casually. Fallen stock near water sources, dogs and wild birds spreads disease. Poultry layer cleanout and litter, manure from sick stock, and aborted material need handling rules.
   Why it matters: environmental fines, disease spread, neighbour complaints, scavenging and bird-borne spread.
   Fix: Fallen-stock SOP that follows the local rule, a designated lined pit or compost bin away from water, post-mortem by the vet for unexplained deaths over a threshold, lime/disinfect, and a record of cause.

11. [MAJOR] Mortality and alert thresholds are undefined, and species-blind thresholds will either not trigger or trigger daily.
   Problem: "mortality spike" has no number. Sheep/goat kid losses of 10-15% may be normal in one system and a disaster in another. Layer flocks, broilers, calves, lambs and adults differ. There is no definition of who is alerted, via what channel, when connectivity fails, or what happens when the owner (a busy academic) does not answer. Cameras and sensors will give alert fatigue, and the plan relies on rural connectivity that is not yet known.
   Why it matters: if the alert fires late, or fires so often it is muted, the "management by exception" model has no exception detection and the owner learns of a disaster from the audit.
   Fix: Set numeric thresholds per class from benchmarks (e.g. poultry daily mortality above about 0.1% for broilers/layers needs a call, a sudden jump or doubling in 24 h is an emergency; adult ruminant 2 or more unexplained deaths in a week; any abortion cluster of 2 or more in a week; any sudden death with no cause). Add a second recipient and a 2-hour no-response escalation (vet or deputy) with the manager authorised to act. Test the alert chain quarterly. Make power-fail and water-fail paper procedures not dependent on a phone signal.

12. [MAJOR] Poultry plan is too vague to be safe, and HPAI/Newcastle handling is nowhere written down.
   Problem: "layers or broilers" is undecided, and the biosecurity rules are listed but not specific. Needs: all-in-all-out, a clean/dirty line, no mixing ages, foot dips with changed disinfectant, wild bird exclusion, rodent control, no sharing of equipment, vaccination (Newcastle, Marek, IBD, coccidiosis and fowl pox by region, plus Salmonella in layers), ventilation failure alarms and generator backup (heat stress and power loss kill whole houses in hours), ammonia and litter control, and a cull-and-compensate rule under HPAI.
   Why it matters: a ventilation or power failure can kill thousands in a night, and HPAI means the whole flock is culled and the premises restricted, which is a total loss without insurance or reporting.
   Fix: Decide species and system first; install temperature and power-failure alarms with an automatic call to two people; backup generator tested weekly; written HPAI report-and-lockdown steps; free-range birds housed on an HPAI order; do not keep poultry near ruminant or pig housing, and do not share staff.

13. [MINOR] Reproduction and breeding plan details are missing or risky.
   Problem: AI vs bull is left open; AI needs a trained technician and heat detection, which a task-sheet farm will not do. Bulls are dangerous. Inbreeding is not controlled, no body-condition targets at mating, no pregnancy diagnosis, no culling criteria, no breeding soundness exams, no abortion investigation. Seasonal breeding for sheep and goats is not planned, and cross-species effects are ignored (ewe/doe nutrition around mating, buck effect).
   Why it matters: low conception, long calving intervals and unproductive animals eat feed.
   Fix: Define a controlled mating window per species, bull selection and handling facilities, pregnancy scanning 40-60 days, BCS targets at mating and before birth, annual cull rules, and an abortion investigation trigger (see item 1).

14. [MINOR] Nutrition and feed planning ignore forage quality, water and seasonal gaps.
   Problem: rations "by class" are listed but no dry-season/drought feed bank, no water-quality test, no mycotoxin and moulds control for stored feed, no plan for poisonous plants, and "feed demand vs crop output" assumes crop residues and grain are available and safe. Ruminant feed bans on animal protein (BSE), urea toxicity, and rodent contamination of stores are not mentioned.
   Why it matters: drought and feed shortfalls are the most common cause of herd losses and forced sales at poor prices.
   Fix: Drought and feed-budget trigger (stock-reduction plan at defined pasture or feed-days remaining), 2 months' feed reserve, feed store SOP, water test, and a plant-poisoning check of paddocks.

15. [MINOR] Handling facilities, housing, fences and water are in "infrastructure" but not specified for each species.
   Problem: goats need much stronger fences than sheep, cattle need a crush and race, a loading ramp, shade, shelter and wet-weather lying areas. Water failure in the dry season kills stock in a day or two, and the plan has a water alert but no manual backup.
   Why it matters: escapes, injuries, road accidents involving neighbours' property, and heat or water-loss deaths.
   Fix: A facility checklist per species, daily water-point check by sight and not only by sensor, a backup water supply and trough, and third-party liability insurance.

16. [MINOR] Hoof, parasite and condition management is a list item with no frequency or trigger.
   Problem: hoof care, footrot (sheep/goat), lice, mange and fly strike, ticks and tick-borne disease (East Coast fever, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, heartwater depending on region), and external parasite dipping are not scheduled. Vector-borne disease needs a regional plan, including vaccination or tick control.
   Why it matters: tick-borne disease and fly strike kill quickly and are region-specific.
   Fix: Ask the vet for a regional disease list and calendar. Build tick control, footrot and fly-strike SOPs.

17. [MINOR] Welfare is only a heading in WP10.
   Problem: no welfare standards or humane-killing procedure. Staff must be able to euthanise a downed or suffering animal promptly, and who is authorised and trained (captive bolt, firearm, or poultry cervical dislocation) is not stated. Transport, loading, heat stress, dehorning/castration/tail docking and pain relief, and weaning stress are not covered. Welfare audits by buyers or certifiers may apply.
   Why it matters: suffering animals left until the vet arrives, and prosecution for neglect.
   Fix: A fit-to-load and fit-to-live decision tree, a euthanasia authority and trained person on every shift, an emergency vet number, and legal limits on mutilations with anaesthetic and analgesic.

18. [MINOR] Dashboard KPIs for livestock are not named, and the wrong KPIs reward the wrong behaviour.
   Problem: KPI-linked incentives could encourage hiding deaths, selling sick stock, or over-using antibiotics.
   Why it matters: bonuses tied to mortality alone encourage unrecorded losses.
   Fix: Use paired KPIs (mortality plus audited stock counts and cause-of-death evidence), weaning percentage, kidding/lambing/calving rate, ADG, FCR, FEC trend, treatment rate, conception rate, and ewe/doe/cow replacement rate. Tie bonuses to audited figures.

19. [MINOR] The timeline puts the hands-off period in months 3 to 6 and ignores the livestock year.
   Problem: a 12-week writing phase and a 3-month shadow cannot cover one full production cycle (a breeding, birth, weaning, dry season and disease season). Untested SOPs go live before a cycle has shown the weak points.
   Why it matters: the first lambing, calving or HPAI season will be the test, and by then the owner has withdrawn.
   Fix: Keep the owner or a hired consultant vet/stockman as a backstop for the first full annual cycle, and gate withdrawal on passing milestones (audit clean for N months, a birth season completed with target survival).

20. [MINOR] Insurance, compensation and market compliance are lightly covered.
   Problem: livestock mortality, poultry disease and liability insurance, certification for slaughter and sale (inspected abattoir, withdrawal compliance), milk/egg hygiene rules, and the handling of the carcass for human consumption are not planned.
   Why it matters: an uninsured disease event or rejected carcass can end the business.
   Fix: Check insurance cover (and whether notifiable-disease compensation depends on timely reporting), slaughter and sale channels with certificates, and food-safety SOPs for milk and eggs.
